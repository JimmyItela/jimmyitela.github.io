Self-Assessment:

Completing the Computer Science program and building my ePortfolio has allowed me to reflect on the extent to which my technical skills and professional objectives have developed throughout my education. Initially, I started the program with a goal of becoming more proficient at software development and extending my technical background related to audiovisual systems, network integration, and technical systems. However, during my time in the Computer Science program, my interest in cybersecurity and information technology has grown, as I realized that there are no standalone concepts such as cybersecurity and data management in software design.
Consequently, my professional goals have evolved to encompass cybersecurity and information technology, where I can combine my software development skills with my previous technical experience in systems and networks. My previous technical experience has taught me to think about the relationships between different parts of a system. The Computer Science program has extended that thinking to the software level, giving me an overall perspective on how modern computing solutions are designed.

One of the skills I have developed in the Computer Science program is my ability to collaborate in a technical environment. Collaboration in a technical field entails more than just dividing the tasks among teammates. To work successfully with others, one needs to create a solution that is easily understood, reviewed, tested, and modified by other developers. The code reviews I had in my capstone project taught me this lesson well, as the issues I failed to spot during my development became apparent during code reviews, including threading-related ones and an edge case in authentication. Instead of regarding this as a deficiency in the process of development, I learned to see this as an opportunity to gain a different perspective and to learn about the assumptions I have made, that others did not share. This experience has solidified my appreciation for modular code, documentation, testing, and good interfaces, as they facilitate collaboration.

The ability to communicate with stakeholders is another skill that I have developed in the Computer Science program. Technical knowledge alone will not help you to solve a problem if it is not communicated to stakeholders in terms they can understand. By writing project documentation, conducting code reviews, completing milestone narratives, and giving presentations, I have learned to convey not just the information about my solution, but also the reasons for certain decisions and the alternatives that I have considered. This is especially true in the case of cybersecurity and information technology, where the risks associated with technology need to be explained to decision-makers.

During my coursework, I have also enhanced my understanding of data structures and algorithms. I have learned that when a developer selects an algorithm, he or she is not just concerned with getting the right result. In addition, a developer needs to consider the efficiency, scalability, edge cases, and the characteristics of the data used. During my capstone, I implemented the ideas of applying a number of algorithms, including sorting time-series data, computing moving averages, calculating statistics, and performing the least-squares linear regression. I also conducted evaluation of the computational complexity and considered the possibility of incrementally caching some computations.

Software engineering has become one of the main areas where I developed skills in the Computer Science program. Early projects used to concentrate on making sure that an application works correctly. With the further development of my coursework, I started to think not only about functionality but also about maintainability, separation of concerns, testing, and modification in the future. I learned to structure my software into layers, with each layer being responsible for a different aspect of the functionality, instead of having presentation, business logic, and persistence mixed up in a jumble of code. Using such patterns as MVVM, repositories, DAO interfaces, dependency injection, and automated unit testing illustrated that architectural decisions can greatly influence testing, maintainability, and modification of the software. Furthermore, my understanding of databases has evolved from mere storage and retrieval to include relational database design, SQL queries, indexing, schema evolution, and data persistence. Among the main lessons that I have learned in this regard was the necessity to account for the presence of existing data when changing the database. For instance, the non-destructive migration I have performed demonstrated the responsibility that developers need to shoulder when dealing with the user's information. Additionally, I gained experience in analyzing the patterns of queries and improving efficiency through indexing and database-side aggregations.

Security has become one of the strongest influences on my way of approaching computing problems. While initially I used to think about security as one of the features of an application, developed after the rest of the application, now I try to understand how the system can fail, how it may be misused, and how its assumptions need to be protected throughout the development process. During my coursework, I had an opportunity to implement secure credential storage, password validation, protection of authentication, defensive handling of inputs, and adversarial testing. Also, the experience of implementing salted password hashing made me realize that security, usability, performance, and architecture need to be taken into account at once.

My ePortfolio brings together all the skills I have mentioned above. Through the improvement of a Weight Tracker Android application, built initially in CS 360, I demonstrate my understanding of the interrelationships of different areas of computer science in software solutions. Instead of presenting three different projects as my artifacts, I chose to illustrate the evolution of one application, in the context of software engineering, algorithms and data structures, and database improvements.

The Software Design and Engineering enhancement establishes the architecture of the improved application. Here, I used such techniques as MVVM and repository pattern, introduced DAO abstraction, separated the business logic from Activities, enhanced authentication, and added the testing. This artifact illustrates my ability to assess the existing application, find the shortcomings, and restructure it using software engineering and security best practices.

The Algorithms and Data Structures enhancement extends the application from mere data storage to data analysis, which makes it even more useful to the user. Here, I added the functionality of analysis of the user's weight history, such as sorting chronological data, computing moving averages and statistics, performing least-squares linear regression and using the trend to estimate progress toward the target. This artifact illustrates my ability to implement an algorithm, account for the problems of invalid or insufficient data, evaluate computational complexity, and present the results to the user.

The Database enhancement completes the evolution of the application. Here, I modernized its persistence layer by migrating it from SQLite to Room while preserving the compatibility with existing data through a non-destructive migration. Moreover, I added the indexing and the database-side aggregation for weekly averages. Due to the software engineering enhancement done previously, the persistence technology can be changed without affecting ViewModels and the presentation layer. This demonstrates how the architectural decisions made in one area can support improvements made in another area. Thus, these artifacts are not just the illustrations of three different enhancements. They show my ability to assess an existing computing solution from different perspectives and to improve it, taking into account the architecture, algorithms, data management, performance, testing, maintainability, and security. Also, the evolution of the Weight Tracker application reflects the evolution of my professional skills throughout the Computer Science program. From concentrating initially just on the functionality, I progressed to evaluating how the solution is designed, how efficient it is, how it safely handles the information, and how maintainable it is.

Completing this program has strengthened my technical skills and my professional orientation. Now I approach computing problems with the better understanding of the relationships between software, infrastructure, data, users, and security. Combining this with my previous experience of systems integration, I am now ready to enter the field of cybersecurity and information technology where such understanding of the whole system is crucial. My ePortfolio represents the technical skills I have obtained, but more importantly, it demonstrates my problem-solving, communication, security awareness, and continuous learning skills that I plan to use in my professional career.
